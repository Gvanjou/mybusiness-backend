## Cafés and small restaurants
Location- and owner-dependent; competition is local (a few hundred metres). Main costs: rent, staff, food cost (often 30–35% of sales in food service), utilities. Margins are thin and very sensitive to rent and owner involvement. Good fit for owner-operators with hospitality experience; rarely passive.

## Bakeries
Similar to cafés but with production: early hours, equipment-heavy, more stable daily demand. Wholesale to shops or offices can add steady revenue. Equipment condition matters a lot for price.

## Hotels and guesthouses
Demand comes from tourists, so location relative to attractions, seasonality and online reviews (booking platforms) matter most. Competition is the same town or resort. Can be semi-passive with a manager, but occupancy varies strongly by season.

## Retail shops
Depend on foot traffic and on competition from chains and online sellers. Inventory value is part of the price; check what is sellable. Owner involvement is usually daily unless staff are in place.

## Car washes and car services
Steady local demand, equipment-heavy, location near main roads matters. Car services depend on skilled mechanics staying.

## Online stores and e-commerce
Location barely matters; competition is national and international (marketplaces, imports). Value lies in traffic sources, supplier terms, brand and customer base. Ask for traffic and sales by channel, return rates and supplier contracts.

## Manufacturing and food production
Competition is regional or national, often with imports. Value depends on equipment, certifications, contracts with buyers (retail chains, HoReCa) and raw material supply.

## Wineries
Competition is by product segment and sales channel (export, retail chains, restaurants, wine tourism). Vineyard ownership, brand, certifications and export contracts drive value.

## Rental property and commercial space
Not an operating business, but often the most passive option. Returns come from rent; check tenant, lease term, vacancy risk and maintenance costs. Yield = annual rent ÷ price.

## Which businesses a beginner should look at first
For a first purchase, the best choice is an operating business that is simple to run and whose results can be verified. Look first at businesses where:
- Operations are simple: a small range, few staff (2–5), no complex kitchen or production. Examples: a coffee point or small café with a simple menu, a bakery sales point, a mini-market, a beauty salon with its own staff.
- Revenue can already be verified: bank statements, POS data, at least 1–2 years of trading.
- The premises are leased on a long, transferable lease, in a location with steady foot traffic.
- The seller will help during a handover period (2–4 weeks) and the staff stay.
- The price is only part of your money: keep 15–25% for renovation, stock and the first months' costs.
Riskier for a beginner: a restaurant with a full kitchen, a bar or nightclub, production, seasonal businesses and anything that depends on the owner's personal connections.
